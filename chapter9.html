APUSH Chapter 9 Study Guide (1815–1828)

Covers By the People Chapter 9, “New Industries, New Politics” — the cotton economy, the market revolution, and the return of political division.

The one-sentence version

Chapter 9 covers the years 1815–1828: the cotton gin and surging European demand turned cotton into the nation's dominant export and exploded the domestic slave trade into the black belt (9.1), the Erie Canal, steamboats, packet ships, and new corporations and banks built a national market economy that also produced the Panic of 1819 (9.2), and the Marshall Court's nationalist rulings, the Missouri Compromise, and the contested elections of 1824 and 1828 turned the Era of Good Feelings into a divided, two-party political system (9.3).

The three objectives at a glance

9.1 — Creating the Cotton Economy pp. 260–266

The cotton gin, the black belt, the domestic slave trade, slave labor and community, and the rise of Lowell's textile mills and factory girls.

9.2 — Commerce, Technology, and Transportation pp. 269–278

New York's rise, the Black Ball Line, the Panic of 1819, the Erie Canal, steamboats and roads, banks and corporations, and the new market economy.

9.3 — From the Era of Good Feelings to the Politics of Division pp. 279–288

The Marshall Court's key rulings, the Missouri Compromise, the contested 1824 election, the American System, and Jackson's 1828 victory.

How the test questions are asked

Remember the Facts easy

Direct recall: what an event was, who did something, what a document or ruling said.

Understand the Connections moderate to hard

Cause and effect, comparisons, and true/false judgment calls. Watch for "All of the following… EXCEPT" and "What distinguished…" questions. Options are kept similar in length on purpose, so you can't guess by which one sounds more detailed.

Strategy for this chapter

  • Keep the three Marshall Court cases of 1819–1824 straight: Dartmouth College v. Woodward (protects charters), McCulloch v. Maryland (protects the national bank from state taxation), and Gibbons v. Ogden (federal power over interstate commerce).
  • Don't confuse the Panic of 1819 (a financial collapse caused by falling cotton prices and tight bank credit) with the Missouri crisis of 1819 (a political fight over slavery's expansion) — they happened in the same year but are unrelated.
  • Know the difference between the Erie Canal (New York State project, completed 1825) and the National Road (federal project, begun 1811).
  • Keep the 1824 and 1828 elections straight: 1824 was decided by the House after no majority (the "corrupt bargain"); 1828 was a popular landslide for Jackson.
  • For EXCEPT questions, check each option true or false against the text, then pick the one that's false.
